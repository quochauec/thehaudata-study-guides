Data Visualization Fundamentals

Why visuals work, the four-step process, how to set the context, and how to pick the right chart. Practice uses the Global Electronics Retailer data from the Lesson 7 homework.

A good visual lets your audience see the answer in seconds. The skill is not drawing charts. It is deciding who needs to know what, then picking the simplest chart that shows it. Try the chart picker in tab 3.

Why What visualization gives you

Faster
  • People spot a top-5 ranking or a gap in a chart far sooner than in a table
  • Quicker decisions
Easier to understand
  • Raw rows become trends, outliers and patterns
  • Works for business, marketing, public health
Easier to spot errors
  • A spike or a hole in a line chart often means a data problem
  • Data visualization appears in 20.7% of analyst job postings (slide source: 365 Data Science)

Where it sits in the process: Lessons 1–6 get and shape the data, Lesson 7 shows it, Lesson 8 onward goes back to deeper analysis.

Process Four steps to an effective visual

  1. Context. Who is the audience, what must they know or do, how will you show it?
  2. Purpose. What are you showing: comparison, change over time, relationship, distribution, part-to-whole?
  3. Choose the chart. Match the chart to the purpose (table below).
  4. Remove clutter. Delete gridlines, borders and effects that carry no message. Highlight the one thing that matters.

Step 1 Set the context: Who, What, How

Ask
WhoAudience and decision maker. Be specific: "the marketing manager", not "management".
WhatThe key message and the action you want.
HowThe data that supports the message, and the visuals that carry it.

Slide case: a badge feature ran for 2 months. You want the marketing manager to approve 6 more months of budget. Who = marketing manager. What = the badges moved the business more than they cost, so invest more. How = transactions and spend per user before vs after, and total gain vs campaign cost.

Tips Bars and lines

  • Bars encode length, so the baseline must start at zero. Order the bars on purpose (largest first, or a natural order) and keep the gaps narrow.
  • Variants: cluster, stacked, 100% stacked, waterfall. Prefer horizontal bars for long labels.
  • Lines show trends. Keep to 4–5 lines. A zero baseline is not required for lines, but is usually wise. Skip it when the range is small and far from zero, when zero is not meaningful, or when values go negative.
  • Combo chart (bars + line): watch the two scales, limit to two metrics, label the legend clearly.

Tips Pie, treemap, table

  • Pie: only for "percent of" with a few slices when the general share matters more than precise sizes. No 3D or exploding slices. Group small slices (80/20), sort by size.
  • Treemap: more than 6 categories or two-level hierarchy. Hard to compare areas exactly. Values must be positive.
  • Table: people read tables and see charts. Use a table when the audience needs several exact values or you are showing an appendix. Drop heavy borders, align numbers, add a light heatmap.

Tips Scatter and bubble

  • Scatter: make overlapping points transparent, label regions, add a legend if you show clusters, drop trendlines you do not need.
  • Bubble: x, y and bubble size. Use it only when the third number earns its place.

Watch out Common mistakes

  • A truncated bar axis exaggerates gaps. Drag the baseline slider in tab 3 and see a 4.6% gap look huge.
  • Too many lines or slices. If you need a legend to read a pie, use a sorted bar chart instead.
  • Chart before context. If you cannot say the key message in one sentence, you are not ready to draw.
  • Two slide notes to read carefully. "Zero baseline" is a rule for bars; for lines it is advice with exceptions. The bubble-chart slide says not to use a bubble when the extra dimension adds little.
