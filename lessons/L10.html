Cohort Retention Analysis with SQL

Group customers by the month they first bought, then track how many come back in each later month. Five SQL steps, a retention heatmap and a reading guide, on TheLook e-commerce data.

Cohort analysis answers "how well do we keep the customers we win?" Group people by when they started, then follow each group over time. See the real heatmap in tab 3.

What Cohort retention

A cohort is a group that shares a starting point, such as the month of first purchase. Retention is the share of that group still active after 1, 2, 3 ... months.

Why Retention pays

Winning a new user usually costs far more than keeping one. Duolingo found that their Current User Retention Rate was a high-leverage metric for daily active users and put a team on it: the longer users stay engaged, the more become paying users.

Read it How to read a cohort table

  1. Cohort month (rows): the month users first bought or subscribed.
  2. Month 0: the starting size of the cohort. Always 100% because it is the base.
  3. Month 1, 2, 3 ...: the share of the original cohort still active that many months later.

Read across a row to see how one cohort decays. Read down a column to see whether newer cohorts retain better than older ones.

Parts Three components of a cohort table

1. The cohort
  • Acquisition month
  • or product version, campaign, promotion
2. Start value
  • Users, orders, revenue or acquisition cost in the first period
3. Retention period
  • The step you track: day, week or month
  • and how many remain after X periods

Method Five SQL steps

  1. First purchase month. MIN(order date) per customer, cut to the month. This is the cohort.
  2. Current purchase months. The distinct months in which each customer ordered.
  3. Retention period. Join steps 1 and 2 and compute DATEDIFF(month, cohort_month, order_month). Period 0 is the first month.
  4. Count per cohort and period. COUNT(DISTINCT customer) grouped by cohort and period.
  5. Retention rate (optional). Divide each count by the period-0 count, with conditional aggregation, to get a cohort table in one query.

Questions Class and homework

  1. Classwork: how well do we retain new users month on month, and what can we learn to improve retention overall?
  2. Homework: for each cohort by acquisition month, any special findings?

Result What the real data shows

On TheLook, about 3.5% of a new cohort buys again in month 1 and the share stays near 3 to 4% for months 2 to 6. There is no steep early drop and no later flattening, which is typical for this synthetic data. Real subscription data usually decays fast in month 1, then levels off.

Watch out Mistakes in cohort tables

  • Incomplete months look like zero retention. A cohort that started 3 months before the data ends cannot have a month-6 value. A raw query shows 0.0, which reads as "everyone left". Show NULL or blank for periods that have not happened yet (see Practice 5).
  • The last month is partial. This data stops on 2024-05-09, so the 2024-05 cohort and month are incomplete.
  • Count distinct customers, not orders. A customer with three orders in a month is one retained customer.
  • Month difference counts boundaries. DATEDIFF(month, '2023-01-31', '2023-02-01') is 1 although it is one day. That is what you want for cohorts, but not for "30 days later".
  • "Retained" needs a definition. Here it means any valid order in the later month. Another team may require a minimum spend or a login.

Check yourself Reading the heatmap

  1. Is retention decaying? Read across a row. The values stay between about 2% and 4%, so there is no steep decline.
  2. Are newer cohorts better? Read down a column. Month 1 moves from 2.5% (Jan) to 3.3% (Jun), within normal noise for 1,300 to 1,700 customers.
  3. What would you do? With about 3 in 100 buyers returning, the bigger lever is converting the first purchase into a second one, for example a follow-up offer in the first 30 days.
