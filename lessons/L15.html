Ask, Define and Structure Analytics Questions

Turn a vague request into a question you can answer, break it into branches that do not overlap, and check each branch with SQL. Practice on the CRM sales pipeline, AdventureWorks and Global Electronics.

Most of the work in an analysis is deciding what to ask. A sharp question saves the queries you would have written for the wrong problem. Try the question workshop in tab 3.

Scope What a data analyst gets asked to do

Requests arrive as ad-hoc messages: a Slack line, an email, a question at the end of a meeting. The job is to turn each one into an answer the business can act on. The slides put the Ask and Share steps at about 20% of the workload for 80% of the result.

Step in a typical processWhat happens
Business understandingLearn how the business makes money and who is asking.
AskTurn the request into a clear analytics question.
Prepare and ProcessFind the data, check it, clean it.
AnalyzeQuery, compare, test.
SharePresent the answer to the people who decide.
ActThe business does something with it.

Framework DIKW

Data becomes information when you add context, knowledge when you add meaning, and wisdom when you add insight. A table of 8,800 deals is data. "63.2% of closed deals were won" is information. Knowing that the rate depends on the month of closing is knowledge. Deciding what the team should do about it is wisdom.

Skills Domain knowledge first

A good question needs the business model, the stakeholders, how revenue and cost arise, the competitors and the key metrics. The slides name six business types in tech: e-commerce, SaaS, free mobile app, media site, user-generated content and two-sided marketplace. Each one watches different metrics.

Research Business Model Canvas and business stage

The canvas puts a business on one page: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, activities, partners and cost structure. The business stage tells you which question matters now.

Anatomy The five parts of an analytics question

PartIt answersExample, from the CRM pipeline
1. Problem and context (and time)Why are we asking, and about which data?The Sales Director thinks win rate is low. The CRM holds 6,711 closed deals from March to December 2017.
2. What, how or whyWhat kind of answer do we need?What is the win rate by period and segment?
3. MetricHow is the answer measured?Win rate = Won / (Won + Lost), on closed deals.
4. ActionWhat will someone do with it?Choose where to coach the team.
5. Goal and timeHow much, by when?A recommendation for the next sales review.

A clear question names a metric and an action. "Why are sales bad?" names neither.

Structure Issue trees and MECE

An issue tree splits one question into branches that together cover it. A good tree is consistent (the same kind of split at each level), MECE and coherent (each branch leads to something you can check). MECE means mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. Two tests:

  • Overlap? Can one item sit in two branches? If yes, the branches are not mutually exclusive.
  • Full cover? Does every item land in some branch? If not, the branches are not collectively exhaustive.
Way to splitIdeaExample

The e-commerce tree on the slides: Revenue = Orders x AOV, and Orders = orders from new customers + orders from repeat customers. Pyramid Principle and SCQA come back in the Storytelling guide.

Watch out Mistakes in a first question

  • Two win rates in one number. Won / closed is 63.2%. Won / all deals is 48.2%. Write the definition into the question (Practice 1).
  • A drop that is a calendar effect. Win rate reads 82.1% in the first quarter and about 61% after it, but that quarter holds only March (Practice 2).
  • A tree that only looks complete. Counting sales by reason adds up to 27,647 for 31,465 orders, because some orders have several reasons and some have none (Practice 4).
  • A join that drops rows without a warning. The product name GTXPro is spelled GTX Pro in the products table, so 1,480 deals disappear (Practice 4).
  • No action. If nobody can say what they would do with the answer, the question is not ready.
  • Starting from the data. Checking what the tables hold is fine. Choosing the question from what is easy to query is not.
